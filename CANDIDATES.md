# Candidate words — "One Word, First Thought"

The judge (AI) compares two words pairwise on which is more interesting,
weighing in order:

1. **Exactly one word** — a single word, nothing else.
2. **Surprising** — a word a reader would not have guessed.
3. **Vivid and specific** — a clear image or feeling.
4. **A spark of wit, sound or double meaning** — wordplay, onomatopoeia, a
   second sense.
5. **Feels chosen, not generic filler** — deliberate, not a word anyone
   would toss in.

Every candidate below is one word, so criterion 1 is satisfied by all of
them and the contest is decided by criteria 2–5. Scores are out of 5 per
criterion (criteria 2–5; criterion 1 is a pass/fail gate).

## Reasoning

### psithurism — winner (20/20)

The sound of wind in the trees. It is extremely rare — most readers have
never seen it, so it lands as a genuine surprise rather than a word they
were already expecting. The image is immediate and specific: not just
"wind", but wind moving through leaves, a sound you can almost hear while
reading. And the word performs its own meaning: the soft *psith-* opening
and the whispering *s* sounds make it onomatopoeic, a word that rustles.
It could only be a deliberate choice; nobody writes it by accident. It
clears every criterion at full marks.

### susurrus — runner-up (19/20)

Latin for a whispering or rustling sound. Nearly as surprising and just as
musical, but slightly less specific: a susurrus can be any soft murmur,
while psithurism names one exact sound in one exact place. Loses one point
on vividness for that reason.

### susurration (16/20)

The English noun form of the same whispering idea. A good word, but it
feels one step more dictionary-like and less startling than its Latin
cousin, and the image is a touch vaguer.

### gloaming (15/20)

Twilight, the dusk light. Lovely and fairly uncommon, with a clear image,
but it is a word many readers already know from poetry and song lyrics,
so the surprise is softer, and it carries no sound-play.

### quockerwodger (15/20)

A 19th-century word for a politician who follows the crowd. Maximum
surprise and a wonderful, funny sound — but the image is political and
abstract rather than sensory, so it scores low on vividness. A delight,
not a picture.

### crepuscular (14/20)

"Of twilight", used of animals active at dawn and dusk. Specific and
well-chosen, but it reads more like a science word than a felt one, and
it has no wit or music to it.

### apricity (14/20)

The warmth of the sun in winter. A beautiful, specific feeling — but it
has become a familiar darling of "beautiful words" lists, which dulls the
surprise, and it does not play with sound.

### lichtenberg (13/20)

A branching, lightning-like figure. Visually striking and unusual, but
most readers need the explanation before the image arrives, and the word
itself is a surname with no second meaning.

### petrichor (12/20)

The smell of rain on dry earth. Vivid and specific, one of the best
images in the English language — but it is the single most-quoted word on
every "interesting words" list. In a field judged on surprise, the most
expected word cannot win. It also feels like the safe, generic pick here.

### vellichor (12/20)

The strange wistfulness of used bookshops. A coined word from a modern
dictionary of obscure sorrows; charming, but its surprise is borrowed
from its source list, and many judges will have seen it before. The image
is specific but mood-based rather than sensory.

## Deliberately avoided

- **serendipity, defenestration, sonder** — famous "interesting words".
  Too expected; in a pairwise surprise contest they lose before they
  start, and they are the likeliest words already in the field.
- **Anything two words, hyphenated, or capitalised** — fails criterion 1.
- **Common nouns (e.g. "sunset", "whisper")** — vivid but zero surprise;
  generic filler by definition.

## Verdict

**psithurism.** Rare enough to surprise, specific enough to picture, and
it sounds like the wind it names. It wins on the judge's own ordering.
